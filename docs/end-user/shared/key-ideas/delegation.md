# Delegation

You care about a cause but you don't have the time or expertise to evaluate every project that claims to serve it. You just want to help.

Delegation lets you do exactly that. You pledge funds (say, $20/month for education in your area) and delegate to someone you trust. Maybe it's a friend who's deeply involved in local education. Maybe it's a well-known figure in the field with a transparent track record. They decide which specific projects your money goes to.

You can check in whenever you want — everything is transparent, you can see exactly where your money went. And you can stop the delegation at any time if you disagree with their choices: any unspent funds become yours to redirect or withdraw. (Funds your delegate has already directed to a project are spent — they can't be clawed back. Revoking only affects what hasn't been spent yet.)

## Why this matters

Most people who want to help don't want a second job evaluating projects. Demanding that every donor read every pitch is a great way to ensure that most people don't donate at all. Delegation lets you contribute on a fire-and-forget basis, with someone whose judgment you trust handling the details.

The alternative most people face today isn't doing all that homework themselves — it's outsourcing the decision to a distant, impersonal layer: a faceless charity org's internal grant process, or a government bureaucrat allocating a budget you never chose. Commonality keeps the convenience of delegation without that impersonality. Your delegate is someone *you personally chose* — a friend, a respected local figure, someone whose public track record you've actually looked at. Efficiency, without blind trust in strangers you never got to pick.

This also solves a problem for project creators: instead of needing to convince thousands of individual donors, they need to convince a handful of delegates who already have pooled funds earmarked for their cause. Donors and busy creators meet in the middle, through people whose actual job (or hobby) is paying attention.

## One hop, not a chain

Delegation goes exactly one hop: from you to the person you chose. Your delegate cannot pass your spending authority on to someone else — someone you may not know and never got to vet.

That's deliberate. Chains of delegation are how “delegation to someone you personally chose” quietly becomes “delegation to whoever a stranger picked.” If your delegate knows someone whose judgment fits your cause better than theirs, they can *suggest* that you delegate to that person instead — but only you can make that change, because only you know whom you trust.

This is the same trade the whole system makes: encourage the crowd to centralize by *one* level — to people who actually follow the ecosystem — but no further. One level buys most of the efficiency of specialists doing the evaluation work, without losing the personal, chosen relationship that makes delegation trustworthy in the first place.

## How this shows up in practice

- As a donor, you pick a delegate when you pledge. You can see their past decisions and track record before committing.
- As a delegate, you direct pooled funds toward projects you believe in. Your track record is public and transparent. You cannot pass spending authority onward; you can only suggest another delegate to the donor.
- Delegation is revocable: you can take your money back, redirect it, or switch delegates at any time.
- No incorporation, no nonprofit paperwork. A delegate is just a person (or organization) that donors have personally chosen to authorize.
