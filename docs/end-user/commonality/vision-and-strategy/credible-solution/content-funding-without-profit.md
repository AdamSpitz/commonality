# Content funding still works with at-cost reimbursement

The original pitch for [content funding](../../../content-funding/index.md) (and [Civility](../../../civility/index.md) in particular) was that creators could make money producing content with a characteristic people are willing to fund, such as being noninflammatory. Since then, [retroactive funding](../why-its-better/retroactive-funding.md) has been capped at reimbursement: later donors repay early contributors exactly what they put in, never more. The cap keeps the system clear of anything resembling a security. Does it break the pitch?

Mostly not. The cap removes a different layer from the one content funding relies on.

## Who the cap applies to

Money can come back to two different groups:

1. **Creators**, who are paid for content they made. This is uncapped. Rewarding an existing post, pool payouts for qualifying work, and future-content rounds all pay creators for their labor.
2. **Early backers and scouts**, who fund a project before it succeeds. This group is capped at cost.

The pitch "creators get paid for writing what people want" depends on (1). The securities concern was about (2): putting money in with an expected return produced by someone else's effort. Payment for your own work is compensation, not an investment return.

> **Open question for counsel:** confirm that a creator can be paid more than their costs for their own content under this structure. The rest of this page assumes that's true.

## What the cap does remove

It removes the **venture layer**. Nobody can earn a profit by spotting promising creators early and fronting them money, so the profit motive for scouting is gone.

For content, that matters less than it sounds:

- **Content is cheap to make, and the creator's own time is the main investment.** Creators don't need financiers. Most of them are already posting.
- **Payment can mostly come after publication.** Rewarding a piece that already qualified involves no speculative funding at all.
- **AI evaluators do most of the scouting.** What remains for human scouts and [delegates](./delegation.md) is mostly curation, and reputation plus a giving budget they can reuse is enough motivation for that.

The real gap is upfront commissions for expensive work, such as a three-week investigative video. Those still work through [assurance contracts](./assurance-contracts.md) (future-content rounds), where backers pay once a goal is met and get no return. That route works; it's just less exciting.

## The real risk is credible demand

The hard question was never the profit cap. It's whether creators believe payment will come. Will someone write the noninflammatory version of a piece *in advance*, betting that it will be rewarded? That depends on:

- **How big the pools look.** A large, visible pool pulls in supply. "Someone might tip you" doesn't.
- **How predictable payouts are.** A committed budget per period (for example, "$X a month split among qualifying pieces") reads like a prize, and creators understand prizes. A finite pool spread thinly over a lot of qualifying content feels like a lottery.
- **Whether the evaluators can be gamed.** Once money follows an evaluator's verdict, creators will write to the evaluator instead of to readers. [Open, swappable evaluators](../../../alignment/ai-evaluators.md) help, but this needs ongoing attention.

## The framing that works

Don't think of content funding as a way for creators to make a living. Think of it as **a reward that tilts which posts creators make.** Political writers already publish every day. If modest, reliable money goes to the version of a post that the other side can actually hear, that shifts what they write at the margin. That needs no investors and no profits, only pools large enough to be visible and payouts regular enough to plan around.

This also makes the cap easy to explain publicly: it's philanthropy for a healthier information commons. Creators get paid for good work, and the people who fund it get recognition, not returns.

Right, the point of content funding isn't really "we need a bunch of scouts looking for promising posts and buying into them early in order to make a profit."

The social-media world is already pretty good at widely-distributing good posts. An unknown creator writes a tweet, it gets retweeted and retweeted up the influence ladder. Not a big deal, happens every day.

Also, AI can do a lot of the legwork. (You can even cheaply submit your tweet to an AI you think will bless it!)

So what we need is an ecosystem of people saying: "Hey, my AI post-suggester is pointing me to (or is configured to automatically pay) a reasonably-popular tweet that is written in a noninflammatory way, and the total value-asked-for on the primary market is reasonable, so a dollar will buy me a decent share of it. Great, it gets a dollar."

The people funding it get recognition, not profit. That's actually probably fine.
