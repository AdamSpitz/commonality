# Something like a network society, without the society

Balaji Srinivasan's network state starts from a real observation: a lot of people who want the same concrete thing (a sugar-free food environment, medicine outside the FDA's menu, a school that teaches a particular curriculum) cannot get it from the institutions they already live under. His path from there is a startup society: one moral innovation, one founder, one commandment, a shared community, a shared treasury, in-person gatherings, and eventually a census large enough to bargain with existing states. (ADAM: I don't mean to strawman Balaji; it's possible that what he's imagining is less specific than that. That's just the picture I got in my mind when I read his book.)

The part worth keeping is the observation and the physical layer. (ADAM: Yes, more or less. What I'd say is that another point that Balaji gets right is that the movement needs a way to collectively fund stuff; what he gets wrong is that that *doesn't* require a big centralized treasury. I guess you've already said that.) People with a shared preference need a front door, a way to find each other, gatherings at a few scales, a memory, a way to trust or drop providers, people who keep the local nodes alive, and a small interface to landlords, venues, and insurers. The purpose changes the directory and the events. Those jobs stay the same, and each society-shaped cluster still has to do them itself.

The part Commonality is built to avoid is the organization those jobs usually get stuffed into. [Easier than politics](../so-what/easier-than-politics.md) already says this: a million-member group with a treasury, a constitution, and a membership boundary is a new government, with the usual fight over who holds the pot. The substitute is many statements, many boards, many assurance contracts, and [organic coalitions](../why-its-better/organic-coalitions.md) that show up where people agree on a concrete outcome and nowhere else. Nobody has to agree on the rest.

Two things follow.

A playbook is possible. The same list of jobs can be written as example LazyGiving projects. Funded together, they produce something that *behaves* like a functioning network society. Nobody ratifies it, and nobody can capture it by taking the treasury, because there isn't one.

And a lot of Balaji's central roles shrink or split. They do not disappear. Someone still writes a good statement. Someone still draws a map. The difference is that a second statement and a second map are normal, not a schism.

## Front door

A stranger still needs somewhere to start. They do not need *the* door.

**Canon.** Balaji wants one commandment, revised on purpose so the loudest chapter does not silently replace it. Commonality wants many commandments. Anyone can write a statement and center a cause board on the projects aligned with it. Leaders who can write a statement other people actually sign are still valuable, and funding that writing is still a real job. What drops out is the requirement that everyone else coordinate on that one text. A chapter that rewrites the commandment in its own words has not broken the society. There was no single society to break. Where two wordings still point at the same project, the implication graph can put that project on both boards. See [organic coalitions](../why-its-better/organic-coalitions.md).

**The site.** Balaji wants the map of chapters, the next events, and the canon, in one place. Here a chapter publishes its own existence if it wants to be found: a place, a time, a statement it cares about. Anyone can publish a map over the chapters that opted in. Tools can build directories off those publications. The job remains — a map nobody maintains is not a map — but "the" map is a product someone can fund, not an office someone has to hold.

**Onboarding.** The path is from "I read a post" to "I know which statement I am willing to sign, which projects that puts in front of me, and who meets near me." There is no society-wide committee that rejects people for wanting the aesthetic. Filtering happens in two smaller places. A cause board only shows projects aligned with what you signed. A local chapter, which is just a small group with a room, can decide who it invites back. That second filter is ordinary hosting, not a membership roll.

## Finding each other

Introductions do not scale by adding more well-connected Alices unless the introductions leave a trail.

**Local matching.** Point a new person at a few people nearby who will actually reply. The fundable version is a month of that work in one city, with a public count of introductions made and the private details left off-chain.

**A directory of providers.** Doctors, restaurants, tutors, employers, hosts, landlords — whoever a given statement's signers might want. The list rots in months. Paying someone to rebuild it is one project. Paying someone else to check it is a second project, and the checker should not be the person who listed the providers. Competing directories are fine. An audit that says which listings are real is what keeps them honest.

**Routing.** "Who do I ask about X" is private judgment. The artifact worth funding is that judgment remaining available after Alice is busy: a maintained page, a standing offer to answer for a month, not a vibe.

## Gatherings

Events are how a pile of signers becomes a population. These jobs look like Balaji's, because a room is a room.

**Local recurring meetings.** A host, a room, a rhythm, and enough facilitation that a newcomer is not stranded. The failure mode is month six, when the first host is tired. Fund the quarter, not "the community."

**Regional gatherings.** Worth traveling for. Programming, ticketing, housing, and a reason to come besides "meet strangers who agree with us."

**The big event.** Once a year, at whatever scale the signers actually support. Norms get demonstrated in public there, so the program and the code of conduct are the product. One event can have one voice for a weekend. That does not make that voice the society's voice.

**Pop-up presence.** A street, a building, or a week in one city where people actually live together. Balaji's archipelago is this job repeated until it has an address. Each repetition can be its own contract and its own lessee.

## Memory and voice

**Internal record.** What a chapter tried, which gatherings happened, which experiments failed. A page with an editor, not a chat scrollback. Each chapter can keep its own. A cross-chapter record is just another publication someone chose to maintain.

**Outbound explanation.** Newsletters, talks, a standing answer to a hostile question. Many people will do this, in different words, and that is the ordinary case. Humans do not agree on everything. The point of the organic-coalition machinery is to let them fund a shared outcome where they agree on *something*, which is the situation the usual institutions handle badly: those institutions often demand agreement on *everything* before anyone can move. A contradictory explainer is not evidence that the arrangement has failed. It is evidence that nobody was forced onto one platform.

What still wants a single voice is a specific artifact: one event, one chapter's house rules, one map's methodology. Fund those. Do not fund a minister of information.

**Translation and local adaptation.** Translate a statement if it helps. Rewrite it if it helps. A city that words things differently has not failed to be a chapter of the original. If the rewrite still implies the same concrete project, both wordings can fund it. If it does not, they are two preferences, and both can exist.

## Trust

Any cluster organized around a preference attracts people extracting status, money, or cover.

**Vouching.** Who will introduce someone, and what they are willing to be on the hook for. This is a local reputation fact. Publishing "I vouch for this host" is enough structure. A global vouching office is not required.

**Dispute handling.** Two people who share a kitchen or a chapter need a person and a procedure, or the fight becomes the chapter's main activity. That person is a local role. It does not generalize to "the society's court."

**Removal.** A small group that meets in person needs the ability to stop inviting someone. Above that scale, the equivalent of removal is unfollowing them, not co-signing their projects, and not listing them. There is no central discussion group to moderate, and no central treasury to take over. That absence is the point of doing this with assurance contracts instead of a pot.

**Directory quality.** A listed clinic, school, or host that is fraudulent is a failure of that listing. It is not a stain on a society, because the listing was never an official endorsement unless someone attested it. The fundable job is the independent check, published where the directory's readers will see it.

## Keeping the doers

A local habit dies when the host burns out and the next person has to invent the first month from scratch.

**Host kits.** How to start in a new city: first ten people, first venue, first three months, and when to stop. One public artifact. Every later city is a use of it, not a new ideology.

**The next host.** Recruit them before the current one quits. This is a role inside one city for one season.

**Succession.** Founder-shaped roles still exist, but they attach to a product, not to a people. The person who edits a particular map, leads a particular annual event, or is the phone number a venue already has. When they leave, fund a handoff of that product. There is no office called "the person outsiders call about the society," and inventing one recreates the founder Balaji's version needs.

## Census

Balaji wants an auditable count of people, income, and real estate, because a society that cannot count itself cannot bargain with a state. Diplomatic recognition is not the goal here. Knowing which efforts are real still is.

**Headcount that is hard to fake.** Tally counts people who signed a statement. LazyGiving counts money pledged and work that actually cleared a threshold. Neither counts a chat membership. Both are harder to inflate than a follower number, and neither pretends to be a citizenship roll.

**Liveness.** Which cities met this month, and which providers are still practicing. Signatures do not show this. A host who publishes "we met" or an auditor who checks a listing does.

**Outcomes of the purpose.** Sugar metrics, case series, school results. Designing the measurement is meta-work. The activity being measured is the meat of the preference, and it gets its own projects.

What this census will not do is produce "the population of the society." It produces overlap: these people signed this, these contracts paid out, these gatherings were published. That is the number a funder can use.

## Interface with the outside

Gatherings, houses, and clinics still sit inside a country.

**Legal wrappers.** Someone has to be able to sign a venue contract, buy insurance, and hold a lease. That someone should be scoped to the event or the house, not to the whole preference. A reusable kit — "here is how a weekend incorporates and gets insured" — is a public good. A single foundation that all chapters must use is the treasury problem in legal costume.

**Permits, insurance, safety** for anything bigger than a dinner. Part of the event's budget, not a standing ministry.

**Deals.** Employers, schools, clinics, landlords. Someone negotiates, then publishes the result into a directory other people can audit. Adjacent preferences can share a provider without merging. A sugar-free dinner and an unwoke school do not need a common commandment to use the same insurance kit.

**Crisis logistics.** A person is stranded, doxxed, or sick in a city where a parallel service exists. The public good is the response path: who to call, what is already arranged. The individual transfer of money to the person in trouble is ordinary help, and it is a bad assurance-contract target.

## What to fund

| Job | What the project actually is | Why a threshold fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A statement people can sign, a host kit, a one-time map, a legal-wrapper kit | A public artifact. Partial funding publishes nothing useful | The artifact either exists for everyone or it does not |
| A city's dinners for one quarter, a regional weekend, one annual event, one pop-up week | Hosting an encounter, budgeted with its own insurance and its own lessee | The gathering happens only if the threshold is met |
| An audit of a directory, a count of which chapters met, a check on a provider's claims | Evidence, published by someone other than the person being checked | An unchecked claim is not worth buying |
| A month of introductions in one city, a month on call as the local host, mediation inside one chapter | A named person's availability for a bounded period, with a public log of what was done | Same shape as a month of software maintenance |
| "People should feel welcomed," a membership committee, a society spokesperson, a shared treasury | Not a project | There is nothing to deliver, or delivering it rebuilds the organization this setup exists to avoid |

## A starter playbook

These are examples for one preference in one city. Swap the purpose and the city. None of them requires the others to exist first, which is the property that makes this a protocol rather than an organization. The ones that are lumpy public goods are assurance contracts. The standing local roles are repeating monthly or quarterly contracts.

1. **Write the statement.** A Tally statement specific enough that a project can align with it. Not a manifesto that settles every neighboring question.
2. **Publish the host kit.** First ten people, first venue, first three months, when to stop calling it a chapter.
3. **Host this city for this quarter.** Room, rhythm, facilitation, a published note that the meetings happened.
4. **Be the introducer for this city for this month.** A public count of introductions. No dossier on the people introduced.
5. **Publish a map** of gatherings and providers whose organizers opted in. Say what was left out on purpose.
6. **Audit that map.** An independent pass over a stated sample. Publish which listings answered and which did not.
7. **Run one regional weekend,** with ticket threshold, housing, program, and insurance inside the same contract.
8. **Publish the one-event legal kit** used for that weekend, so the next city does not hire the same lawyer from scratch.
9. **Hand off a role.** One contract whose deliverable is that a named next host, editor, or event lead has everything the last one had.
10. **Measure the purpose,** separately. Whatever the preference claims to change in the world. That project cites the gatherings and the directory. It does not define who is a member.

Items 3 through 6 are the ones a treasury-Discord usually extracts from unpaid status. They are also the ones that decide whether two people in the same city ever meet. The purpose-specific meat — the clinic, the school, the sugar program — hangs off that, and can be funded by anyone whose statements reach it, including people who would never join a society together.
