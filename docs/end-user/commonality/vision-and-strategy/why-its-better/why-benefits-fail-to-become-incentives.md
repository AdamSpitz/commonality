# Why benefits fail to become incentives

A writer could make a careful argument that people on the other side would
actually consider, or publish another dunk. Many people would benefit from the
careful argument: readers who want to understand, people who want their views
represented fairly, and everyone who would prefer a less hostile public
conversation. But those benefits do not necessarily pay the writer. If engagement
rewards the dunk, producing the dunk may be the individually sensible choice.

[Civility](../../../civility/index.md) tries to supply the missing reward. People
who want better discourse can fund the kind of writing they want to exist,
including writing addressed to someone else. The writer can respond to that
demand without first persuading a social-media platform to change its business
model.

This is an instance of a much broader problem:

> People would benefit from better decisions or useful work, but their willingness
> to pay never becomes a credible reward that reaches the people who could deliver it.

That describes both missing work and the wrong mix of work: maintenance versus
another feature, replication versus another novel finding, or a public reference
versus knowledge kept inside one business. The person doing the work need not be
uncaring or irrational. The benefit to others simply fails to reach their choice.

Commonality's opportunity is to let dispersed beneficiaries turn some of that
benefit into funding. The practical test is: **Whose choice changes once this
money can reach them—and why?**

## Sometimes the funding opportunity has not been imagined

“I wish social media were less inflammatory” is a familiar desire. “There should
be money available for anyone who produces a good, noninflammatory explanation”
is a further step. Someone can have the first thought without ever having the
second. They may think of better discourse as something to wish for, ask platforms
to encourage, or practice themselves, without thinking of it as something they
could help pay others to produce.

In that case, the obstacle comes before finding contributors or creators. There
is no concrete funding opportunity in people's heads, and no shared place to
point to. Naming the desired quality, spelling out what would count, and attaching
actual funding makes an otherwise vague wish actionable:

- “Here is where you put money to make more of this happen.”
- “Here is where you find out how your work could get paid for making it happen.”

A page alone cannot do that. It needs a connection between the stated purpose,
available money, judgments about qualifying work, and a route to payment. But
giving that connection a visible home matters: people can share it, add to it,
and plan around it. Creating the funding destination is itself organizing work.

This differs from **missing discovery**. Sometimes an opportunity already exists
and people cannot find it. Here, someone must first formulate the opportunity:
turn a quality people value into something they recognize as fundable. A useful
founder question is: **What do people wish existed that they have never thought
of attaching a funding pool to?**

## A crowd paying a crowd for a kind of value

Ordinary crowdfunding makes it easy to imagine many people paying for one
specified project. Supporting a creator makes it easy to imagine many people
paying one person for an ongoing body of work. There is another useful pattern:
many people funding a kind of value, with many independent people able to produce
qualifying work. The contributors need not select every producer in advance,
and each producer need not assemble a separate paying audience.

[Content Funding](../../../content-funding/index.md) makes this concrete. People
can fund content with explicitly stated qualities, and different creators can
produce work that serves that demand. A contributor might care about careful
explanations addressed to a particular audience without already knowing who
should write them. A creator might be able to write one without already having
fans willing to support their career.

The organizing commitment is “more work of this kind,” which can outlast any
particular piece or producer. The pool gives both sides a common reference point:
contributors can see what they are helping make possible, and producers can see
what people are willing to fund. Individual payments still go to particular
recipients; the crowd need not act as a single contractor.

Here, “pool” describes a shared funding purpose, not a requirement that everyone
surrender their money to one administrator or accept one judge. In Commonality,
people can choose whose evaluations and spending decisions to trust. Nor does
qualifying automatically entitle every submission to payment: a credible
opportunity must explain how limited funds are allocated, when payment happens,
and which decisions remain discretionary. A visible budget is a signal of demand;
a commitment to pay for specified work is a stronger promise.

The general pattern also appears in prizes, bounties, and grant programs. The
opportunity is to make it easier to establish and sustain for neglected kinds of
value, including repeated contributions by many producers.

### Beyond social-media content

Possible applications of the same coordination pattern include:

| Shared funding purpose | Work different people could contribute | What evaluation would need to establish |
|---|---|---|
| Make public information accessible in a neglected language | Translations of useful guides, supplied by different translators | Accuracy, usefulness to the intended readers, and permission to publish |
| Keep a shared collection of open-source tools usable | Reproduced bugs, accepted fixes, or maintained documentation across many tools | That the work solves a real problem and is accepted or useful, rather than manufactured activity |
| Improve an open map's accessibility information | Verified updates about entrances, lifts, and step-free routes | Accuracy, freshness, and whether an update adds information rather than duplicating a paid claim |
| Make published research easier to check | Independent reproductions of analyses, with data and methods available | Methodological quality and independence, including when results fail to replicate |

These are possible extensions of the funding pattern, not claims that Content
Funding already verifies or pays for these activities. Some work needs funding
before it begins; some can be rewarded after delivery. Each domain needs its own
evidence and allocation rules.

“Post proof that you planted a tree” exposes the same distinction. A post is
evidence to assess, not the value itself: someone must establish that the tree
was appropriately planted, survives, and has not been claimed repeatedly. Making
a pool visible addresses a missing coordination point; it does not make real-world
outcomes easy to verify.

## How this fits the existing strategy

[What kinds of public goods is Commonality better for?](./what-its-better-for.md)
catalogues many types of institutional blockers. This page supplies a complementary
diagnosis: where does the connection between benefit and incentive break?
[Unbundle the organization](./unbundled-organization.md) explains how different
people can supply money, judgment, trust, distribution, and execution. The
[cause taxonomy](/specs/product/cause-taxonomy.md) turns these considerations into
varied, concrete projects.

The distinction matters because a good cause title is not enough. “Science” does
not tell us which choice needs to change. “Pay a research team to perform and
publish a replication even when the result is negative” does.

One source of broader inspiration is [Moloch's Toolbox](https://equilibriabook.com/molochs-toolbox/) by Eliezer Yudkowsky,
which distinguishes decision-makers who are not beneficiaries, asymmetric
information, and coordination traps. Those distinctions also set limits on the
funding thesis. Sometimes the missing ingredient is trustworthy knowledge;
sometimes several actors must change together. More money reaching one person
does not automatically resolve either problem.

## Where the connection breaks

These categories overlap. A project can encounter several at once.

| Why the incentive fails to arrive | How Commonality might help | What remains unresolved |
|---|---|---|
| **The value has no funding destination.** People wish for more of something without imagining it as a kind of work they could pay for. | Give the desired value a name, explicit criteria, and a visible connection to actual funding and a route to payment. | A page and a good label do not supply contributors, workable criteria, or credible payment terms. |
| **No single producer to commission.** The desired value consists of many contributions from people who may not yet be known. | Let a shared funding purpose direct money to qualifying work from many independent producers. | Limited funds need allocation rules; eligibility alone cannot promise payment to everyone. |
| **Dispersed benefits, concentrated costs.** Many people benefit, but none can justify commissioning the whole thing. | Aggregate contributions toward a concrete deliverable, such as a maintained shared dependency. | Beneficiaries can still choose to free-ride. Total social benefit is not the same as money people will contribute. |
| **Uncertainty about others contributing.** People would help if enough others did; a partially funded attempt may buy nothing useful. | Assurance contracts make contributions conditional on reaching a funding threshold. | Reaching the threshold does not itself guarantee delivery or quality. |
| **Missing discovery.** Beneficiaries cannot find each other or someone willing to do the work; producers cannot see demand. | Visible funding intentions and fundable-projects boards help supply and demand find each other. | A founder still needs to reach people. Publishing a board does not create an audience. |
| **Costly evaluation.** Each person's benefit is too small to justify independently researching every proposal or result. | Chosen delegates and attesters let people rely on others' judgment; evaluation work can itself be funded. | Evaluators can be wrong or captured. A public record is not proof of truth. |
| **Competing rewards favor the worse choice.** Attention, prestige, or sales reward behavior that benefits the wider constituency less. | Add a revenue source for the neglected behavior: civil argument, replication, maintenance, or open documentation. | The additional reward must be credible and large enough to change the relevant choice. Money may not compensate for lost status or institutional access. |
| **Value is recognizable only afterward.** Early contributors bear uncertainty while later beneficiaries enjoy the result. | Retroactive donations reimburse early contributors at cost, allowing the same giving budgets to fund another attempt. | Later donations are uncertain. Reimbursement offers no profit and cannot by itself attract people seeking an investment return. |
| **No shared institution.** Beneficiaries cross jurisdictions or distrust one another's organizations. | People can co-fund a specific deliverable while choosing their own delegates and sources of judgment. | The shared deliverable must actually be acceptable to them; discovering overlap cannot manufacture agreement. |
| **Several actors must move together.** Going first is costly or useless without complementary changes. | Finance shared transition costs, enabling infrastructure, or a viable alternative. | A money threshold is not a binding agreement that several institutions will adopt a new practice together. |
| **Beneficiaries cannot pay or participate.** The benefit accrues to future people, people without resources, or other absent stakeholders. | Motivated sponsors can contribute on their behalf. | The system still depends on someone present being willing and able to pay. |

Assurance contracts address a particular obstacle to cooperation: “I would
contribute if enough others did.” They do not eliminate every reason to free-ride.
Similarly, delegation lowers the cost of participating but does not make judgment
infallible, and retroactive reimbursement recycles giving capacity without
guaranteeing it will be replenished.

There are also cases where the decision-maker cannot legitimately accept the
proposed payment, lacks authority to change the practice, or faces a legal
barrier. A fundable implementation project or independent alternative may help;
a generic offer to pay for a better decision is not sufficient.

## Why Civility is a particularly useful example

Civility illustrates **a missing funding destination**, **many producers serving
a shared funding purpose**, and **competing rewards**, beyond merely collecting enough money
for an otherwise uncontroversial project. Its hypothesis is that a constituency
for better discourse can finance a different mix of content from the mix that
engagement rewards alone would produce.

There are at least two interested groups: people who want to read the other
side's views without being insulted, and people who want their own side's views
to receive a fair hearing. Neither group needs to adopt the other's politics to
fund the same piece. Audience-specific evaluators help assess whether the piece
can be heard by its intended readers; people still choose whose judgments to
trust and what to fund.

The proposed causal chain is:

1. People express demand and commit money toward a kind of content.
2. A creator can see a credible funding opportunity.
3. The creator chooses to produce something that serves that demand.
4. Evaluation helps contributors identify qualifying work and direct funding.
5. Repeated funding makes producing this kind of work a more viable practice.

Each link matters. Paying for existing content demonstrates recognition, but not
necessarily additional production. A respectful tone does not establish factual
accuracy. Funding publication does not establish that the intended audience saw
it, understood it, or changed its mind. A useful account of a project should
say which links it exercises and which remain hypotheses.

## Questions for a proposed project

For a concrete project, a reader should be able to answer:

- **Beneficiaries:** who gains, including people who will not pay?
- **Funding destination:** has anyone made this kind of value explicit and given people a place to fund it and find paid opportunities?
- **Decision-maker:** whose choice determines whether the benefit happens?
- **Producers:** is this one commissioned project, or an ongoing invitation for many people to contribute qualifying work?
- **Current choice:** what do they do now, and what rewards or constraints explain it?
- **Missing connection:** why can't beneficiaries already reward the better choice?
- **Intervention:** which mechanism — pooled funding, an assurance threshold, delegation, evaluation, or reimbursement — changes that situation?
- **Credibility:** what makes the funding opportunity believable before the work begins?
- **Allocation:** if more work qualifies than the budget can cover, who gets paid, how much, and who decides?
- **Evaluation:** who judges delivery, by what evidence, and what could fool them?
- **Boundary:** what remains unsolved even if the money arrives?

The test is the same question from the start of this page: **Whose choice
changes once this money can reach them—and why?**






# Chat with Astra


> Any suggestions for additions we could make to the product or the fake-data examples or whatever? Like, does this give you any ideas for cool new coordination mechanisms or ways we could use the existing ones or whatever?


Yes. The most interesting shift is to make **“there is money available for more of this” a first-class product experience**. Right now, the docs describe the ingredients, but we could make their combination much more tangible.

I’d explore these six ideas. The first two mostly assemble existing mechanisms; the others introduce new behavior.

1. **Give every funding opportunity a producer-facing view.**

   A cause board could answer: “What could I do here to get paid?” Show the desired qualities, examples of qualifying work, available funding, who makes funding decisions, and how to propose work.

   Put **“Fund more of this”** and **“Produce something like this”** beside each other. That makes your two-sided coordination point visible. It should distinguish money people can currently direct from money actually committed to a particular piece of work.

2. **Let people turn an example into a standing invitation.**

   After funding a useful piece, offer: **“What about this would you like more of?”**

   Someone funds an explanation of a contentious issue, then specifies: “Explains the strongest opposing argument accurately, for readers unfamiliar with it.” They can find an existing matching cause or publish that funding purpose and attach money.

   This provides a path from *recognizing value afterward* to *requesting more beforehand*. Examples and counterexamples could also help evaluators interpret the criteria.

3. **Let producers ask for a commitment before doing the work.**

   A visible pool may attract twenty people to write essentially the same explainer. Only three get funded; the rest learn that responding to the incentive was a gamble.

   Allow a lightweight proposal: “I could cover this topic for $200.” A contributor or delegate can then fund a prospective round using the existing assurance mechanism. A more ambitious extension would reserve part of a budget until a delivery deadline.

   The new coordination benefit is **allocating effort before people spend it**, while preserving a route for unsolicited completed work.

4. **Fund the missing pieces of a collection.**

   Sometimes “more qualifying work” stops being useful because everyone tackles the easiest example. A pool could publish gaps: explanations for these five audiences, translations into these three languages, accessibility information for these uncovered locations.

   Contributors still fund a shared purpose; producers choose gaps they can fill. Completing a gap changes what the pool seeks next. This would require additional tracking and allocation design, but gives the money a way to express *what is still missing*.

5. **Make “this would be fundable if someone could evaluate it” actionable.**

   Suppose people want to fund accessible translations but nobody trusts the available quality assessments. The board could expose that bottleneck and invite a separate funded proposal for evaluation work.

   That creates a useful sequence: contributors express demand → a reviewer establishes usable criteria and assesses submissions → producers can respond to a credible opportunity. Existing projects, delegation, and attestations provide much of the machinery; the product addition is making the dependency visible.

6. **Try a pool for maintaining a condition over time.**

   For example: “Keep these public guides accurate as circumstances change.” Different people identify stale sections, make corrections, and review updates. Recurring funding supports continuing usefulness.

   This is an interesting extension beyond rewarding discrete output: sometimes the value people want is **that something stays good**. It needs explicit review intervals and responsibility boundaries.

For fake data, I’d start with **one pool, several strangers, two rounds**:

- Several contributors fund careful explanations for a specified audience.
- Three previously unrelated creators discover the opportunity.
- One gets a prospective project funded; another submits existing qualifying work.
- A third produces something that qualifies but receives no funding because the budget is exhausted.
- In the next round, visible gaps and funding decisions guide what creators propose.

That would extend the existing incentive-demonstrations plan beyond one writer changing their choice. It would demonstrate **a shared funding purpose coordinating several independent decisions**, including where the coordination fails.

My first picks would be the producer-facing view and that synthetic story. They would make the core idea concrete and reveal which additional mechanism is actually needed.
