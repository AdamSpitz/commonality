# Cooperation without a shared platform

A conservation group, a farmer, and a fishing club all want a polluted stretch of river cleaned up. They disagree about plenty of other things. They may even disagree about why the cleanup matters. But they can each help fund the same work.

They don't need to settle those other disagreements, join one organization, or adopt a common political platform. Each decides whether this particular undertaking is worth supporting.

This is one of the opportunities Commonality is meant to create: let cooperation happen at the scope where people actually agree.

## Separate decisions let more people cooperate

Supporting a political party or a broad organization often means accepting a package. Someone may strongly support one part, reluctantly tolerate another, and oppose a third. The package forces them to decide which concerns outweigh the others.

Separate statements and separately fundable projects give people more independent choices. They can support the river cleanup, oppose a proposed development, and fund a local arts event without finding a group that shares all three positions.

The cleanup can draw on the union of people who want that work for different reasons. It doesn't require everyone to agree across the intersection of an entire platform. Each project can have a different collection of contributors, and their participation need not create any broader alliance.

That doesn't make incompatible plans compatible. People still need to accept what the particular project will do. If one proposed cleanup closes the river to fishing and another preserves fishing access, that difference matters. Nor does adding together supporters of separate projects establish support for funding all of them as a package.

## Let the scope of support speak for itself

A contribution to the cleanup is a contribution to the cleanup. It isn't an endorsement of every opinion held by the operator or the other contributors. Likewise, signing a statement expresses agreement with that statement, not with everyone else who signed it.

This should be the ordinary meaning of participation, so people don't have to repeatedly explain every unrelated disagreement. A project page has reason to show the work, its cost, the people responsible, and relevant judgments about it. It usually has no reason to display contributors' views on unrelated issues.

Sometimes the social context still makes an explanation useful. Someone might want to say, “I'm funding this cleanup because I fish here; I still disagree with the organizer about development.” An optional way to attach such an explanation is a possible product extension, not a prerequisite for cooperation or an implemented feature promised here.

An explanation also doesn't add conditions to the money. A contribution follows its actual funding terms; a personal note cannot create a new refund or spending restriction.

## How this relates to bridge-building

Sometimes separate funding decisions are enough. People independently find the same work worthwhile, and their contributions converge without a negotiation.

Sometimes a [bridge](./organic-coalitions.md) helps them discover the overlap, clarify an acceptable implementation, or express reservations. More explicit agreements are useful when participants need reciprocal commitments or intend to make a public alliance. Those are additional options; ordinary cooperation shouldn't require them.

This approach can help useful work happen before a broad coalition is viable. It doesn't require a national majority, only sufficient support for the particular undertaking. It also doesn't replace decisions about public authority or make every policy dispute privately fundable.

The strategic opportunity is to give people more places where they can cooperate without carrying their entire political identity into the decision. Successful work may lead to stronger relationships, but the work remains valuable even if its contributors never join a common movement.

Related ideas: [Individualization](./individualization.md) preserves people's distinct preferences and judgments; [unbundling the organization](./unbundled-organization.md) separates the jobs of funding, evaluating, distributing, and doing the work. This page separates the issues on which people cooperate.
