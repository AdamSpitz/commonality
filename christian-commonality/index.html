Koinonia · a sketch

Some good works are too big for one church and too small for an institution.

So they don't happen. Not because anyone decided against them — because "together" has always meant building an organization first: a committee, a treasurer, a governing body, an argument about who's in charge. That costs more than most good works are worth.

This is a way for a few thousand people who will never meet to fund one specific thing, together, and then go their separate ways.

The whole mechanism

“I'll put in $200 — if enough others do.”

If enough others do, everyone's money moves at once and the work gets funded. If they don't, nothing moves and you keep yours. You can never end up the only one who gave.

One case, to fix the shape in your head

Sixty people can't. Four thousand could. They have no way to find each other.

Picture a church you've driven past. Built in 1889. The congregation is down to about sixty, most of them over seventy. The organ needs $180,000 of work it isn't going to get, and the roof is next.

Those sixty people cannot raise it. That much is obvious, and everyone involved has quietly accepted it.

Here is the part that isn't obvious. Somewhere in that region there are maybe four thousand people — Baptists, Catholics, Presbyterians, a fair number who don't attend anywhere — who would be genuinely sorry to see that building go. At forty-five dollars each, it's covered.

Not one of them will ever be asked. There's no list of them. They don't know about each other. And each one, if you did somehow reach them, would reasonably assume that someone with more standing in the matter must already be handling it.

Nobody said no. There was just never a way to say yes together.

Thirty years of that is why it's crumbling. And once you've got that shape in your head — a real want, spread thin across people who can't see each other, with no institution whose job it is to convene them — you start finding it everywhere.

How it works

Churches can act together without merging.

The whole mechanism is one old idea made enforceable: a promise that only comes due if enough other people make the same promise.

1

Someone names a specific work, and a number.

A church, a ministry, three friends. “$180,000 for this organ.” “$120,000 to keep this shelter open through the winter.” No application, no committee, no grant cycle.

2

People pledge — conditionally.

You promise your part only if the rest comes in. Until then your money stays yours. This is the piece that dissolves the old worry, “why should I, when others won't?” — because if others won't, you didn't.

3

It funds, or it doesn't, and everyone can see which.

If the number is reached, every pledge moves at once. If it isn't, every pledge is released. And whichever happens, the record is public — not a glossy annual report you take on faith, but the actual ledger.

You don't have to read every line.

Give $10 a month, point it at a pastor, a missions veteran, a friend whose judgment you trust — let them do the picking, and never think about it again. Take it back whenever you like.

Nobody agrees to anything but the work.

Fifty churches across three denominations can fund one translation together and go back to disagreeing about baptism on Monday. There's no doctrinal treaty, no shared board, and nothing left standing afterward to fight over.

What it could fund

Ten more of the same shape.

The point of any one of these isn't that you'd personally fund that exact thing. It's that you can picture it, that it's plainly worth doing, and that when you go looking for who's stopping it, there's nobody to name.

So each one carries an honest note on why it's stuck.

Build

Things that don't exist yet, and would serve everybody once they did.

A free Greek and Hebrew study stack

Interlinears, morphology, lexicon tools — the layer underneath every Bible app, every seminary course, every serious study group.

Everybody uses it; nobody owns the bill. It serves all of Christendom, which means it's no church's job.

Church software, built once and shared

Giving, membership, childcare check-in — done properly, so five hundred small congregations stop each paying separate rent for a worse version.

Every church wants it to exist, and every church would rather a different church paid for it.

Translation for a language with 40,000 speakers

Scripture, and good commentary alongside it, for a people group too small to make anyone's portfolio math work.

Too small for an agency's math. Not too small for the several thousand people who actually care.

Serve

Needs that cross more churches than any one leader can convene.

Disaster response, fifty churches, one agreement

It forms when the storm hits and dissolves when the work is done. No permanent bureaucracy, no brand to sustain, no overhead in the quiet years.

Being ready together has always meant funding a standing organization through the years when nothing happens.

Refugee co-sponsorship across nine churches

Including the unglamorous coordination — so nobody double-covers one family and nobody quietly drops another.

Nine churches, no shared authority, and the only tool anyone offers for “together” is a committee.

Recovery housing that outlives the county grant

Fund it outright — or post a standby promise that fires only if the grant is cut, so the house never goes dark while everyone scrambles.

There's no ordinary way to make a binding promise that costs you nothing unless the bad thing happens.

Reach people outside the church

For the audience that currently finds Christianity false, stupid, and immoral.

Writing a skeptic reads to the end

Not softer arguments — better-delivered ones. A standing pool for the people who can make the case to someone who starts out hostile, without giving them a reason to stop reading in the first paragraph.

The feeds pay for outrage and donor bases pay for preaching to the choir. Almost nobody funds the patient version that works.

Paid afterward, for the thing that actually changed minds

You can't pick that in advance. You can absolutely recognize it a year later — and pay the person who made it. By their fruits you shall know them, with a budget attached.

Money has to be committed up front, so it goes to whoever wrote the best proposal rather than whoever did the most good.

Hold on to what's being lost

Kept short on purpose. A list that's mostly grievances only attracts people who already agree.

A named legal case

A church zoning fight, a foster agency's licence, a nurse's conscience claim. One specific case, funded by the people who care about that case.

It's the first thing an opponent wants defunded and the last thing anyone budgets for in advance.

A standby pool against debanking

“If this ministry loses its payment processor, this money covers the move.” Cheap to promise, and most valuable in the years it never has to be spent.

A promise like this is only worth anything if it's credible, and there's no ordinary way to make one credible.

The honest part

What's not on the list — and the question you're already asking.

Half of what makes a list like this credible is what it refuses to claim.

Your own church's roof

The pastor stands up, the plate goes around, and it works — because everyone in that room already knows and trusts each other. Nothing here improves on that. Use the bookkeeping if you like it; it isn't the pitch.

Anything a good ministry already does well

If a competent organization is doing the work and can reach the people who'd fund it, competing with them to prove a point would be vanity, not stewardship.

Anything one trusted org can handle anyway

If the answer to “who has to say yes?” is “one org — and it will,” all we'd add is overhead. This is for the works where the answer is nobody.

“Isn't this the crypto and AI stuff I'm skeptical of?”

Fair question, and honestly: yes. Underneath, the conditional pledge is enforced the same way cryptocurrency is, and AI is used to read mountains of material and surface the rare good piece. You would never have to touch either one. The plan is that it feels like any normal giving app — see the work, see what's been done before, give with a tap, get a receipt.

The blockchain part is just why the books can't be cooked and the account can't be frozen — which matters, because ministries do get quietly dropped by payment processors. The AI part is just why you don't have to wade through the slop yourself, and its instructions are open for anyone to read and argue with.

Three caveats, straight up. (1) The smooth, non-scary version described here still has to be built; this is what it will feel like, not what ships today. (2) A Christian should build and voice this — an outsider ventriloquizing the faith would ring false, and you'd hear it. (3) The works on that list clearly clear the bar. Whether any of this scales up to reshaping the wider culture is a genuine open question, and we're not going to pretend otherwise.
