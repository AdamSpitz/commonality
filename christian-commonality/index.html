The good works nobody has organized

Here is a list of things the church could fund together.

A free Greek and Hebrew study stack that every Bible app quietly depends on and nobody pays for. Five hundred small congregations each paying separate rent for the same church software. A Scripture translation for a language with forty thousand speakers. An organ in a church of sixty that four thousand people in the region would miss.

None of these are blocked by a villain. Ask "who has to say yes, today, for this to happen?" and the honest answer is nobody in particular — it just never gets organized. That's the whole problem, and it's a solvable one.

The board

What would actually be on it.

Read this as a menu of shapes, not a roadmap. The point of any single line isn't that you'd personally fund that exact thing — it's that you can picture it, and that when you ask who's stopping it, there's no one to name.

Every entry below carries an honest note about why it's stuck today. If a good work isn't stuck, it doesn't belong here; see what's not on the board.

Build

Make a thing that doesn't exist yet.

A free, maintained Greek and Hebrew study stack

Interlinears, morphology data, lexicon tools — the layer under every Bible app, every seminary course, every serious study group.

Why it's stuck — everybody uses it, nobody owns the bill. It benefits all of Christendom, which means it's no single church's job.

Translation into a language with 40,000 speakers

Scripture, and the good commentaries alongside it, for a people group too small to make anyone's portfolio math work.

Why it's stuck — too small for a big agency. Not too small for the several thousand people who genuinely care.

A shared church-admin stack

Giving, membership, childcare check-in — built once, well, so five hundred small congregations stop each paying software rent separately.

Why it's stuck — every church would be better off if it existed, and every church would rather another church paid for it. Classic.

A classical Christian school in a town that has none

Specifically the founding year — before tuition covers anything, when the whole thing lives or dies on whether the first cohort shows up.

Why it's stuck — it doesn't fit any grant category, and there's no institution whose job it is to say yes.

Serve

Meet a need directly — often across more churches than any one leader can convene.

Recovery housing that outlives the county grant

Fund it now — or post a standby promise that fires only if the grant gets cut, so the house never goes dark while everyone scrambles.

Why it's stuck — nobody can plan around a budget line they don't control, so nothing gets planned at all.

Refugee co-sponsorship across nine churches in one city

Including the unglamorous coordination work, so nobody double-covers one family and nobody quietly drops another.

Why it's stuck — nine churches, no shared authority, and the only tool anyone offers for "together" is a committee.

Regional disaster response, fifty churches, one agreement

It forms when the storm hits and dissolves when the work is done. No permanent bureaucracy, no brand to sustain, no overhead in the quiet years.

Why it's stuck — the only way to be ready together has been to build a permanent organization, which is expensive and nobody wants to run.

Captioning and live translation of services

So deaf congregants and immigrant families can actually participate — done once at a quality no single small church could afford.

Why it's stuck — each congregation's need is too small to justify the cost; together it's obvious.

Persuade

The column this project cares most about — and the one that connects to something bigger than the church. Why, in Scripture ↓

A standing pool for writing that reaches people who think Christianity is false, stupid, and immoral

Not softer arguments — better-delivered ones. Writing that a skeptic reads to the end instead of writing off in the first paragraph. The Areopagus, crowdfunded.

And here's the surprising part: secular moderates can help fund this exact work, because it's built to be heard rather than to insult. Their money helps make the Christian case, and the act of giving it is itself a small peace made across the divide.

Why it's stuck — the feeds pay for outrage, and donor bases pay for preaching to the choir. Almost nobody pays for the patient, winsome version, which is exactly the version that works.

A working scientist who is a Christian, paid to write for a general audience

On faith and science, at length, for people outside the church — a full-time job nobody currently offers.

Why it's stuck — there's no institution whose budget line this is, in either direction.

Rewards, after the fact, for the apologetics video that actually changed minds

You can't pick these in advance. You can absolutely recognize them a year later — and pay the person who made it.

Why it's stuck — funding has to be committed up front, so it goes to whoever writes the best proposal rather than whoever did the most good.

Translating the good apologetics into languages that have none

The serious books and courses exist in English. Whole national churches have nothing comparable in their own tongue.

Why it's stuck — the audience is real but scattered, and no publisher's math closes.

Local religion journalism

The boring, accurate, non-outrage version — what's actually happening in the churches, schools, and charities of one city.

Why it's stuck — ad-funded media won't (no clicks) and donor-funded media won't (no axe to grind).

Defend

Deliberately kept short. A board that's mostly grievances attracts only the already-convinced — and these rails work best when outsiders can join in.

A named legal case

A church zoning fight, a foster agency's licence, a nurse's conscience claim. One specific case, funded by the people who care about it.

Why it's stuck — legal defense is exactly the thing a hostile authority most wants defunded.

A standby pool against debanking

"If this ministry loses its payment processor, this money covers the migration." Cheap to promise, and most valuable when it's never spent.

Why it's stuck — there's no way today to make a promise that only costs you if the bad thing happens.

Preserve

Keep something from being lost — usually by people who won't be around to see the benefit.

A specific historic building, or an organ

The congregation is down to sixty people and can't fund the repair. Four thousand people in the region would be genuinely sorry to lose it.

Why it's stuck — those four thousand have no way to find each other, and each one reasonably assumes someone else will handle it. Thirty years of that is why it's crumbling.

Digitizing a denomination's archives and hymnody

Scanned, transcribed, indexed, and released for anyone to use forever.

Why it's stuck — the beneficiary is posterity, and posterity doesn't pledge.

And the ones nobody thinks to put on a board

The coordination work itself is a good work, and it's the most underfunded one of all.

Fund the bridge-finder

Someone whose actual job is locating the concrete projects that different denominations can fund together without first agreeing on why.

Why it's stuck — it's nobody's job, so it's nobody's job.

Fund a delegate

A regional missions veteran, paid to decide where pooled money goes, with a public track record anyone can inspect and anyone can stop following.

Why it's stuck — good judgment is the scarce input, and we treat paying for it as overhead instead of as the point.

Fund the auditor

Someone who goes and checks whether a funded project actually delivered, so that "by their fruits" has receipts behind it.

Why it's stuck — verification benefits every future giver and no current one.
The same board, three cities

It isn't one list. It's a shape that fills in differently wherever you are.

And what's blocking the work changes too, which is worth seeing plainly.

Toronto

Pluralist on paper, secular in its institutions. Campus chaplaincy at a large public university, a legal clinic for zoning and accreditation fights, a winter warming centre. What's blocking it: hostile gatekeeping, and needs that span a whole metro area with no one in charge of it.

Paris

Around one percent practising. Planting a church here isn't routine ministry — it's a small, committed minority funding something out of its own pocket. And the historic buildings are tangled up with the state. What's blocking it: too few people, each of whom can't act alone.

Lagos

Large, growing, short on money — and much of the money arrives from the diaspora abroad. What's blocking it: verification. Did the money arrive? Did the thing get built? Open receipts and paying for proven results carry the weight here.

The honest exclusions

What's not on the board.

Half of what makes a list like this credible is what it refuses to claim.

Your own church's roof

The pastor stands up, names the need, the plate goes around, and it works — because everyone in that room already knows and trusts each other. Use these rails if the bookkeeping appeals to you. But it isn't the pitch, and we'd be lying to say otherwise.

Anything a good existing ministry already does well

If a competent organization is already doing the work and can reach the people who'd fund it, competing with them to prove a point would be vanity, not stewardship.

Anything that needs one trusted organization anyway

If the answer to "who has to say yes?" is "one org — and it will," then all we'd add is overhead. The whole value here is for the works where the answer is nobody.

Why the Persuade column is the heart of it

So much of the New Testament is instructions for getting along with people you disagree with.

Not agreeing with them — getting along with them, and reaching them, while you still disagree. Everyone else has to be talked into "engage the other side charitably." You don't. You're already under orders to cultivate it, and you've watched the loudest, angriest version of your faith dominate every feed instead, because outrage is what the feeds pay for.

1 Peter 3:15
"Always be prepared to give a reason for the hope that is in you — yet do this with gentleness and respect."

Make your case, and the manner is part of the obedience. A true argument delivered with contempt has failed the instruction.

Matthew 5:9
"Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called children of God."

Not peace-keepers. Peace-makers — the ones who go into a divide and build something across it.

James 1:19
"Let everyone be quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to anger."

"Listen and engage," written two thousand years early. It's the whole posture in nine words.

Ephesians 4:15
"Speaking the truth in love."

Not truth without love (that's cruelty). Not love without truth (that's flattery). Both at once — the hard part.

Colossians 4:6
"Let your speech be gracious, seasoned with salt, so you may know how to answer each person."

Tailor the delivery to the listener. That's exactly what a good noninflammatory filter does.

Acts 17:22–28
Paul at the Areopagus: he credits what the Athenians got right, quotes their own poets, then makes his case.

The most hostile forum in the ancient world, and he doesn't call them stupid. The noninflammatory move, modeled by the apostle himself.

"Listen and engage" is the civic version. "Love them" is the bigger thing — and it includes telling people hard truths.
So don't hear this as "tone it down, never make anyone uncomfortable." Loving someone means being willing to say the hard thing ("faithful are the wounds of a friend"). The catch is that a hard truth delivered as an insult never lands — the listener just hears "you think I'm stupid and evil" and tunes out. Koinonia doesn't soften your message. It funds the delivery that gets it through the door, so the truth you're carrying can actually be heard. You still supply the truth.
Why it fits the church specifically

This isn't a generic giving app pointed at Christians.

Several things about how the church already works make these rails fit it better than almost any other community on earth.

You already trust the right people

Every congregation already has the network this needs: a pastor, elders, deacons you'd actually trust to direct a gift wisely. Hand them that authority directly — and take it back anytime — instead of mailing a check to a distant office and hoping. That's what makes "fund a delegate" plausible here and nearly impossible elsewhere.

By their fruits you shall know them

You don't have to gamble on a glossy proposal. Fund the ministry that has already borne fruit. The people who spotted it early and took the risk get repaid by those who fund it after it's proven. The talents get invested, not buried.

Keep doing what you're doing

You already give generously, mostly through the church. These are rails for that, not a replacement — the bookkeeping is handled, the ledger is honest and open. Nobody's asked to stop tithing and start trading anything.

No bank to freeze, no platform to ban

When a ministry gets quietly dropped by a payment processor, the question isn't "is there a better system?" — it's "is there any system?" These rails can't be deplatformed, because there's no central account to cut off.

How a line on the board becomes a funded thing

Churches can act together — without merging.

Your congregation can rally around a need in a single Sunday. Yet ten churches that agree on almost everything still can't fund one thing together — not without a committee, a denomination, a treasurer, and a fight about who's in charge. That gap is why most of the board above has never been built.

You don't have to merge the rooms to act together in the hall.
Fifty churches across three denominations want the same translation, shelter, or disaster response funded. They pool their gifts toward that one work, then go their separate ways. No permanent org, no one in charge, no doctrinal treaty. It funds what you already agree on and leaves the rest alone.
1

2

People pledge — conditionally. You promise your part only if the rest comes in. If the goal isn't met, your money is never taken. No risk of being the only one who gave.

3

The threshold is reached, and only then does it fund. Everyone gives together or no one gives at all. The free-rider worry — "why should I, when others won't?" — simply dissolves.

5

The honest part

"Isn't this the weird crypto/AI stuff I'm skeptical of?"

Fair question. Yes — underneath, this runs on the same technology as cryptocurrency, and it uses AI to do the legwork of reading mountains of content and surfacing the rare good piece. But you'd never have to touch any of that. The plan is for it to feel like any normal giving app: you'd see causes and creators, see what they've done, give with a tap, get a receipt. The blockchain part is just why the books can't be cooked and the account can't be frozen. The AI part is just why you don't have to wade through the slop yourself — and its instructions are open for anyone to read, and you can pick whose judgment to trust or ignore it entirely.

Three honest caveats: (1) the smooth, non-scary version described here still needs to be built — this is what it will feel like, not what ships today. (2) A real Christian should build and voice this; an outsider ventriloquizing the faith would ring false. (3) Don't over-promise. The works on that board clearly clear the bar; whether this scales up to reshaping the wider culture is a genuine open question worth being humble about.
