Good works nobody has organized

Sixteen things the church could fund together.

A free Greek and Hebrew study stack that every Bible app quietly depends on and nobody pays for. Five hundred small congregations each paying separate rent for the same church software. Scripture translated for a language with forty thousand speakers. An organ in a church of sixty that four thousand people in the region would miss.

Nothing is blocking any of these. Ask "who has to say yes, today, for this to happen?" and the honest answer is nobody in particular — it just never gets organized.

The list

Read it as a menu of shapes, not a plan.

The point of any single line isn't that you'd personally fund that exact thing. It's that you can picture it, that it's plainly worth doing, and that when you go looking for who's stopping it, there's nobody to name.

So each one carries an honest note about why it's stuck today.

Build

Make something that doesn't exist yet.

A free, maintained Greek and Hebrew study stack

Interlinears, morphology data, lexicon tools — the layer underneath every Bible app, every seminary course, every serious study group.

Why it's stuck — everybody uses it, nobody owns the bill. It benefits all of Christendom, which means it's no single church's job.

Translation into a language with 40,000 speakers

Scripture, and good commentary alongside it, for a people group too small to make anyone's portfolio math work.

Why it's stuck — too small for a big agency. Not too small for the several thousand people who genuinely care.

A shared church-admin stack

Giving, membership, childcare check-in — built once, well, so five hundred small congregations stop each paying software rent separately.

Why it's stuck — every church would be better off if it existed, and every church would rather a different church paid for it.

A classical Christian school in a town that has none

Specifically the founding year, before tuition covers anything, when the whole thing lives or dies on the first cohort showing up.

Why it's stuck — it fits no grant category, and there's no institution whose job it is to say yes.

Serve

Meet a need directly — usually across more churches than any one leader can convene.

Recovery housing that outlives the county grant

Fund it now — or post a standby promise that fires only if the grant gets cut, so the house never goes dark while everyone scrambles.

Why it's stuck — nobody can plan around a budget line they don't control, so nothing gets planned at all.

Refugee co-sponsorship across nine churches in one city

Including the unglamorous coordination, so nobody double-covers one family and nobody quietly drops another.

Why it's stuck — nine churches, no shared authority, and the only tool anyone offers for "together" is a committee.

Regional disaster response — fifty churches, one agreement

It forms when the storm hits and dissolves when the work is done. No permanent bureaucracy, no brand to sustain, no overhead in the quiet years.

Why it's stuck — the only way to be ready together has been to build a permanent organization, which is expensive and nobody wants to run it.

Captioning and live translation of services

So deaf congregants and immigrant families can actually participate — done once, at a quality no single small church could afford alone.

Why it's stuck — each congregation's need is too small to justify the cost. Put together, it's obvious.

Reach people outside the church

Making the case to people who currently find Christianity false, stupid, and immoral — in a way they can take in rather than bristle at.

A standing pool for writing that skeptics actually read

Not softer arguments — better-delivered ones. The kind a critic reads to the end instead of writing off in the first paragraph. The Areopagus, crowdfunded.

Why it's stuck — the feeds pay for outrage and donor bases pay for preaching to the choir, so almost nobody funds the patient version that works.

A working scientist who is a Christian, paid to write for a general audience

On faith and science, at length, for people outside the church. A full-time job that nobody currently offers.

Why it's stuck — there's no institution whose budget line this is, in either direction.

Rewards, after the fact, for the video that actually changed minds

You can't pick those in advance. You can absolutely recognize one a year later — and pay the person who made it.

Why it's stuck — money has to be committed up front, so it goes to whoever writes the best proposal rather than whoever did the most good.

Local religion journalism

The boring, accurate, non-outrage version: what's actually happening in the churches, schools, and charities of one city.

Why it's stuck — ad-funded media won't (no clicks) and donor-funded media won't (no axe to grind).

Protect what's being lost

Kept short on purpose. A list that's mostly grievances only attracts people who already agree.

A named legal case

A church zoning fight, a foster agency's licence, a nurse's conscience claim. One specific case, funded by the people who care about that case.

Why it's stuck — legal defense is exactly the thing a hostile authority most wants defunded.

A standby pool against debanking

"If this ministry loses its payment processor, this money covers the move." Cheap to promise, and most valuable when it never has to be spent.

Why it's stuck — there's no ordinary way to make a binding promise that only costs you if the bad thing happens.

A specific historic building, or an organ

The congregation is down to sixty people and can't fund the repair. Four thousand people in the region would be genuinely sorry to lose it.

Why it's stuck — those four thousand have no way to find each other, and each reasonably assumes someone else will handle it. Thirty years of that is why it's crumbling.

Digitizing a denomination's archives and hymnody

Scanned, transcribed, indexed, and released for anyone to use, forever.

Why it's stuck — the beneficiary is posterity, and posterity doesn't pledge.
What they have in common

Every one of them needs more people than a church, and fewer than a denomination.

There's no villain

Nobody decided against these. There's simply no one whose job it is to decide for them — and no address to send the money to if you wanted to.

Your church can already do this at its own scale

The pastor names the need, the plate goes around, it works. That machinery just doesn't extend past the room. Ten churches that agree on the need still have no way to act on it together.

"Together" has only ever meant an institution

A committee, a treasurer, a governing body, an argument about who's in charge — plus permanent overhead. That price is too high for most good works, so they don't happen.

How a line on the list becomes a funded thing

Churches can act together without merging.

You don't have to merge the rooms to act together in the hall.
Fifty churches across three denominations want the same translation, shelter, or disaster response funded. They pool their gifts toward that one work, and then go their separate ways. No permanent organization, no one in charge, no doctrinal treaty. It funds what you already agree on and leaves the rest alone.
1

Someone names a work. A church, a ministry, anyone. "We need $120,000 to keep this shelter open through the winter." No application, no committee approval.

2

People pledge — conditionally. You promise your part only if the rest comes in. If the goal isn't met, your money is never taken. You can't end up the only one who gave.

3

Everyone gives together, or nobody gives at all. Which is what dissolves the old worry: "why should I, when others won't?"

4

Or hand the picking to someone you trust. Don't want to read every line? Give $10 a month, point it at a pastor or a friend whose judgment you respect, and never think about it again. Take it back whenever you like.

5

It's all visible. Every gift and every disbursement, plainly recorded — not a glossy annual report you take on faith, but the actual ledger, open to anyone.

The honest part

What's not on the list — and the question you're already asking.

Half of what makes a list like this credible is what it refuses to claim.

Your own church's roof

The pastor stands up, the plate goes around, and it works — because everyone in that room already knows and trusts each other. Use these rails if the bookkeeping appeals to you, but it isn't the pitch.

Anything a good ministry already does well

If a competent organization is already doing the work and can reach the people who'd fund it, competing to prove a point would be vanity, not stewardship.

Anything one trusted organization can handle anyway

If the answer to "who has to say yes?" is "one org — and it will," all we'd add is overhead. The value here is for the works where the answer is nobody.

"Isn't this the weird crypto and AI stuff I'm skeptical of?"

Fair question, and yes — underneath, this runs on the same technology as cryptocurrency, and it uses AI to read mountains of material and surface the rare good piece. You'd never have to touch any of that. The plan is for it to feel like any normal giving app: see causes and creators, see what they've done, give with a tap, get a receipt. The blockchain part is just why the books can't be cooked and the account can't be frozen — which matters, because ministries do get quietly dropped by payment processors. The AI part is just why you don't have to wade through the slop yourself, and its instructions are open for anyone to read.

Three caveats, straight up. (1) The smooth, non-scary version described here still has to be built — this is what it will feel like, not what ships today. (2) A Christian should build and voice this; an outsider ventriloquizing the faith would ring false. (3) The works on that list clearly clear the bar. Whether any of this scales up to reshaping the wider culture is a genuine open question, and we're not going to pretend otherwise.
