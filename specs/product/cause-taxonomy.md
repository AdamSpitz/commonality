# Cause taxonomy: how to populate a vertical's board

A vertical founder's hardest early problem is not mechanics, it's **the empty
board**. You've picked an audience, you can say what the site is for in one
sentence — and now you need twenty concrete causes that make a visitor go "oh,
*that's* what this is." This file is the generator for that list.

Related but different:

- **[what-its-better-for.md](/docs/end-user/commonality/vision-and-strategy/why-its-better/what-its-better-for.md)**
  organizes public goods by *what's blocking them today* (seven failure modes of
  government and charity). That's the **gate** — it tells you whether Commonality
  is load-bearing for a given cause at all.
- **[use-cases.md](use-cases.md)** organizes by *what a human arrived wanting to
  do*. That's the **path** — which shipped surface a cause enters through.
- This file organizes by *what the cause is*. That's the **variety** — the facets
  you turn to generate a board that feels rich instead of repetitive.

You need all three. The gate without the facets gives you seven abstract
categories nobody can picture. The facets without the gate give you a hundred
plausible causes, most of which a church bake sale or an existing charity already
handles perfectly well.

## The gate: is Commonality actually load-bearing?

Before a cause earns a slot on the board, name which blocker from
[what-its-better-for.md](/docs/end-user/commonality/vision-and-strategy/why-its-better/what-its-better-for.md)
it's hitting:

1. Self-financed minority good (free-rider problem *within* the niche)
2. Polarization-trapped / hidden majority
3. Scale-mismatched (too small for government, or spanning jurisdictions)
4. Gatekept / unconventional
5. Proven-but-unpredictable (retroactive funding territory)
6. Politically disfavored / suppressible
7. Bridging / communication good

If you can't name one, you have a fine cause that doesn't need us. Put it on the
board anyway if it's *rails* for something the audience already does (see
[rails.md](/docs/end-user/commonality/vision-and-strategy/ease-of-adoption/rails.md)) —
but don't lead with it, because it doesn't demonstrate anything.

## The eight facets

### 1. Subcause — the *why*

The values-level topic. This is the dimension founders always think of first, and
it's the least useful for generating variety, because it's the dimension where
your audience already knows what they care about.

Sub-axis that *does* matter: **doctrinal load** — how much of the *why* a funder
has to share in order to fund the *what*. Low-load causes are fundable across a
whole movement's internal divisions; high-load causes are fundable only inside one
faction. This is the axis the implication graph and
[organic coalitions](/docs/end-user/commonality/vision-and-strategy/why-its-better/organic-coalitions.md)
operate on, and the reason a movement with a hundred factions can still act.

### 2. Scope — the *where*, and *how many parties must agree*

Geography, but the structural version. Tiers, roughly:

- **Single local body** (one congregation, one chapter, one neighborhood)
- **Town / city**
- **Region — multiple bodies, no shared authority**
- **National**
- **Global / diaspora**
- **Non-geographic community of interest** (a profession, a language group, a
  hobby, an online community)

The reason to separate this from "Toronto vs. Paris" flavor: **each tier fails for
a different reason, and the middle tiers are where legacy institutions are worst.**
The bottom tier already works — a leader stands up and names the need, and dense
local trust does the rest. The top tier works too, via a handful of big national
orgs that become brands and gatekeepers. The **region tier — many peer bodies with
no shared authority** — is the one with no mechanism at all, because the only tool
legacy-land offers for "together" is *institutional union*, and union founders on
the fraction the parties don't share.

So: when a cause is at the bottom tier, you're selling rails. At the region tier,
you're selling something that does not currently exist. Weight the board
accordingly.

### 3. Deliverable — the *what you get for the money*

The strongest variety generator. Turn this dial hardest.

- **Content / media** — writing, video, podcasts, translation
- **Software / technical infrastructure** — tools, datasets, hosting, the boring
  shared stack
- **Physical thing** — a building, a repair, equipment, a park
- **A person's time** — a salary, a stipend, a fellowship, someone's sabbatical
- **Service delivery** — relief, care, teaching, counseling
- **Event** — a conference, a festival, a one-off gathering
- **Legal action** — a defense fund, a specific case, an intervention
- **Research / scholarship**
- **Institution-building** — starting a school, a clinic, a press
- **Movement plumbing** — the coordination work itself: finding common ground,
  vetting projects, auditing results, being a delegate

That last one is worth calling out. **Coordination labor is itself an
underfunded public good**, and it's the one nobody thinks to put on a board.
Someone has to do the work of finding what the factions agree on, checking whether
a funded project delivered, and exercising judgment about where pooled money goes
— see D1/D2 in [use-cases.md](use-cases.md#d-route-money-through-people-you-trust)
and [successful-projects.md](successful-projects.md). A board with a row for "fund
the delegate" and "fund the auditor" is a board that can bootstrap itself.

### 4. Posture — the *stance*

- **Build** — make a thing that doesn't exist
- **Serve** — meet a need directly
- **Persuade** — change minds
- **Defend** — protect something under threat
- **Preserve** — keep something from being lost

Founders routinely mix this dimension up with #3, producing lists like "advocacy
projects, infrastructure projects, social media content" — which is two
deliverables and one posture. Keeping them separate is what lets you notice that
you can build *or* defend *or* preserve using any deliverable.

**Strategic note on posture mix.** A board dominated by **defend** rows reads as a
grievance list, and a grievance board attracts people who already agree and repels
everyone else. That's fatal for anything hoping to be co-funded across a divide,
and it's in tension with the recognition-over-persuasion copy voice
(see [csm/](/docs/founder/csm/)). Defend rows are often the most *urgent* and
should be there — but lead with build and serve.

### 5. Time shape — the *when money moves*

- **One-shot** — a threshold, a deadline, done (LazyGiving; use-cases §A)
- **Ongoing stream** — a standing pool or monthly pledge (Content Funding,
  recurring pledges; §B)
- **Contingent / standby** — "if X happens, this money appears." The credible
  threat and the defunding rescue. Cheap to signal, powerful as deterrence;
  see [composability.md](composability.md)
- **Retroactive** — fund what already demonstrably worked (§A4)

Most founders default every row to one-shot. A board with all four shapes
represented teaches the mechanism as the visitor scrolls.

### 6. Beneficiary — the *who benefits*

- **In-group** (the audience funds something for itself)
- **Out-group** (the audience funds something for people outside it)
- **The commons** (anyone, including people who'd never fund it)
- **Posterity** (people not yet here — archives, endowments, heritage)

This one governs **who else can co-fund**. In-group goods are funded by the
in-group and nobody else. Out-group and commons goods can attract money from
people who don't share the *why* at all — which is the entire hidden-majority and
bridging story (#2, #7 above). If every row on your board is in-group, your
board's ceiling is your movement's size.

### 7. Contestedness — the *who objects*

- **Uncontested** — nobody's against it, it's just unfunded
- **Contested by the wider culture** — funders face social cost
- **Contested inside the movement** — high doctrinal load; factional
- **Contested by the state or by platforms** — defunding, deplatforming, debanking

This determines whether censorship-resistance and pseudonymity are decorations or
the whole reason someone showed up
([censorship-resistance.md](/docs/end-user/commonality/vision-and-strategy/hard-to-stop/censorship-resistance.md),
[privacy-slider.md](privacy-slider.md)). It also interacts with #6: a cause that's
*uncontested* and *out-group-benefiting* is the one that recruits outsiders, and
every board should have several.

### 8. Publicness — is it actually a public good?

- **Pure transfer** — money to named individuals. Real, good, and mechanically
  boring: no free-rider problem, so assurance contracts add nothing.
- **Club good** — non-excludable within a bounded group (a shared tool that 500
  small organizations all need). The free-rider problem is *internal*, which is
  exactly category #1's sweet spot and the most underrated cell on the whole grid.
- **Genuine public good** — non-excludable, non-rival. Content, software,
  research, a fixed bridge.

Boards over-represent pure transfers because transfers are emotionally legible.
Keep some, but know that they don't demonstrate the mechanism.

## How to use this

Don't enumerate the grid — eight facets is thousands of cells and most are boring.
Instead:

1. **Fix** the subcause list (#1) to your audience's actual concerns. Five to
   eight of them.
2. **Sweep** deliverable (#3) and posture (#4) hard — these produce the felt
   variety.
3. **Deliberately spread** across scope (#2), time shape (#5), and beneficiary
   (#6), so the board teaches the mechanism rather than repeating one row.
4. **Gate** every candidate: name the blocker. Cut the ones where the honest
   answer is "a competent existing charity does this fine."
5. **Check the mix**: mostly build/serve, at least three region-tier rows, at
   least three rows an outsider could co-fund, at least one movement-plumbing row,
   all four time shapes present.

A useful sanity test on any single row: **"who has to say yes, today, for this to
happen — and why won't they?"** If the answer is "nobody in particular, it just
never gets organized," you've found a good row.
